\begin{abstract}
  We predict coronary heart disease (MICHD) from 321 health and
  lifestyle features of the 2015 BRFSS survey ($328\,135$ training
  respondents, $8.8\%$ positive). We implement the six required methods
  with NumPy only and compare them under one protocol. Because the
  classes are strongly imbalanced we optimize the $F_1$ score and choose
  the decision threshold on held-out data. The only preprocessing step
  that clearly helped was recoding the survey's ``don't know'' and
  ``refused'' answer codes ($+0.013$ $F_1$, better in all five folds);
  squared terms, missing-value indicators and longer training did not
  help beyond noise. Our final regularized logistic regression reaches
  $F_1 = 0.425 \pm 0.006$ in 5-fold cross-validation.
\end{abstract}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death, and early detection
from lifestyle and health information could support prevention. We
use the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a US
telephone survey~\cite{brfss}, to predict whether a respondent
reported coronary heart disease or a heart attack. The task is binary
classification with a rare positive class. Our goals were to implement
the basic methods of the course correctly, to build a careful
evaluation protocol, and to test which preprocessing decisions
actually matter, using ablations rather than intuition.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The training set has $N=328\,135$ respondents and $D=321$ features;
the test set has $109\,379$ respondents. Only $8.8\%$ of the training
labels are positive, so a classifier that always answers ``no disease''
reaches $91.2\%$ accuracy while detecting nobody. Accuracy is
therefore a misleading metric, and we use the $F_1$ score, the
harmonic mean of precision and recall of the positive class
(\pending{state the metric of the competition leaderboard}).

The data is incomplete: $44.8\%$ of all values are missing, $82$
features are complete and $147$ are missing in more than half of the
respondents. Most features are coded answers rather than measurements
($20$ features have at most two distinct values, $199$ have three to
ten, $102$ more than ten). Inspecting the most frequent values showed
special codes: yes/no questions also contain $7$ (``don't know'') and
$9$ (``refused''), and the ``days in the last month'' questions use
$88$ for ``none'' (the most frequent value of the physical and mental
health features), with $77$/$99$ for ``don't know''/``refused''.
Read as numbers, these codes act as large outliers.
Some features describe the survey process (interview date, sequence
number, sampling unit) and not the respondent.

\section{Models and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Implemented methods}

Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ be the feature matrix and $w$ the
weights. The linear methods minimize
$L(w)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_n (y_n-x_n^\top w)^2$. Gradient descent (GD)
updates $w\leftarrow w-\gamma\nabla L(w)$; stochastic gradient descent
(SGD) uses one random sample per step; least squares solves
$X^\top Xw=X^\top y$; ridge regression adds $\lambda\|w\|^2$, which
gives $(X^\top X+2N\lambda I)w=X^\top y$.

For labels $y\in\{0,1\}$ logistic regression minimizes the mean
negative log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
  L(w)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigl[\ln\bigl(1+e^{x_n^\top w}\bigr)-y_n x_n^\top w\Bigr],
\end{equation}
with gradient $\frac1N X^\top(\sigma(Xw)-y)$ and plain gradient
descent. The regularized version adds $\lambda\|w\|^2$ to the objective,
i.e.\ $2\lambda w$ to the gradient; this is useful because the
unpenalized weights diverge when the data is separable
(lecture~5b)~\cite{cs433}. For both regularized methods the returned
loss excludes the penalty. We evaluate the loss in the stable form
$\ln(1+e^s)=\text{logaddexp}(0,s)$ to avoid overflow. All methods use
only NumPy.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

All statistics are learned on training rows only and then applied
unchanged to validation and test rows, so no information from held-out
data enters the model. (1) \emph{Recoding of special codes}, per
feature: if its largest value is $9$, $99$, $999$ or $9999$ we treat it as
``refused'' and set it to missing, together with the matching ``don't
know'' code ($7$, $77$, $777$, $7777$) when present; the codes $88$, $888$
and $8888$ (``none'') are set to $0$. The rule fires on $234$ of the $321$
features. (2) We drop the nine administrative features (state code,
interview date, sequence number, sampling unit, \dots), the features
that are missing in more than $50\%$ of the rows, and constant
features. (3) Remaining missing values are replaced by the median.
(4) Features are standardized, and a constant column is added as bias.
The result has $163$ columns. Labels are converted from $\{-1,1\}$ to
$\{0,1\}$ for training and back for the submission file.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Following lecture~4a we use $M$-fold cross-validation ($M=5$, fixed
seed), fitting the preprocessing inside each fold. Since a model that
outputs probabilities almost never exceeds $0.5$ when only $8.8\%$ of
the labels are positive (at threshold $0.5$ we measured $F_1=0.17$), we
treat the decision threshold as a hyperparameter and choose the one that
maximizes $F_1$. Configurations are compared on the same folds, because
the per-fold \emph{differences} vary much less than the scores
themselves. To estimate how much tuning the threshold on the scored rows
inflates $F_1$, we also report an ``honest'' value: the threshold is
chosen on one half of a validation fold and scored on the other half
(and vice versa). The final threshold is chosen on the pooled
out-of-fold predictions of all $328\,135$ rows, which is more stable than
a per-fold choice.

\emph{Methods.} Table~\ref{tab:methods} compares the six methods. The
five that work reach $F_1$ between $0.399$ and $0.412$; the gaps are
within the noise of a single split (about $0.005$ to $0.008$ from fold
to fold), so we cannot rank them. SGD diverged with step size $10^{-3}$
(weight norm above $10^{9}$), presumably because single-sample
gradients on standardized data with outliers are large; $10^{-4}$ and
$10^{-5}$ were stable. We use regularized logistic regression as our
main model since it models the label directly and is robust to
unbalanced data (lecture~5b).

\emph{Ablation.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows the effect of each
change on a single split. In 5-fold cross-validation, recoding the
special codes improves $F_1$ by $0.013$ and is better in all five
folds; it is the only change that helps. Removing the administrative
features, adding indicators for features with at least $5\%$ missing
values ($79$ indicators), adding squared terms for $58$ continuous-looking
features, and training longer each change $F_1$ by less than the
fold-to-fold noise. Our final setting reaches $F_1=0.425\pm0.006$
in cross-validation ($0.422\pm0.006$ with the honest threshold
estimate), so tuning the threshold on the scored rows inflates $F_1$ by
only $0.001$--$0.003$. Table~\ref{tab:cv} gives the cross-validated
scores of each configuration and their paired differences to the final
setting.

\section{Discussion}

\emph{Strengths.} The pipeline is simple, reproducible and cheap
($72$ seconds from the raw files to the submission), and every design
decision is backed by a cross-validated comparison. Recoding the
special codes is a cheap, interpretable fix that targets a real
property of survey data.

\emph{Why the score plateaus.} Several unrelated changes leave $F_1$
near $0.42$. In the bias--variance picture of lecture~4b this suggests
that our model class is the limit (high bias): all methods fit a
linear function of the same features. The imbalance is also why the five
working methods are so close: the threshold search compensates for the
sensitivity of the least-squares fit to class proportions that lecture~5a
describes (a hypothesis we did not test separately).
More flexible inputs did not help in the form we tried: squared terms
changed $F_1$ by less than the noise.

\emph{Limitations.} We did not test one-hot encoding of the
category-like features, which would remove the false assumption that
answer code $3$ is ``more'' than code $1$, nor non-linear models or
other losses. Our special-code rule is a heuristic and applies to many
columns; some of them may be misclassified. Four survey-design variables
(three sampling weights and a stratum code) are still among the
features; we did not test removing them. The standard deviations over
five folds are only a rough noise level.

\section{Summary}

We implemented and compared the six required methods on BRFSS data. The
decisive choices were to evaluate with $F_1$ and a tuned threshold, and
to recode the survey's special answer codes, which improved $F_1$ by
$0.013$ in every fold. Regularized logistic regression on $163$ columns
reaches $F_1=0.425\pm0.006$ in cross-validation. \pending{Add the
leaderboard result.} The ablations show that the remaining ideas we
tried are within noise, pointing to a richer model class or encoding as
the next step.
